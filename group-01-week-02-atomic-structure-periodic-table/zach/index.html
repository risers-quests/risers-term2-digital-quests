Day 1 · Full Body Scan

What Every Atom Is Actually Built From

You can't map a body without knowing what every organ actually does. This briefing covers the full picture: the particles every atom shares, the one number that proves identity, and exactly why the particles sit where they do.

Why matter is never really solid

Every material thing around you — this desk, the air, your own hand — is built out of atoms, the basic building block of all matter. An atom isn't a solid little ball; it's a tiny, organized structure with different parts sitting in very different places, doing very different jobs, the same way your own body has different organs doing different jobs. Before you can map your own atom-body, you need to know exactly what's already inside every single atom, no exceptions.

1The three particles every atom is built from

Every atom, no matter which element it turns out to be, is built from the same three subatomic particles. The proton carries a positive charge and sits packed inside the nucleus, at the atom's center — your heartbeat, the identity that drives everything else. The neutron carries no charge at all — neutral — and sits right alongside the protons, also in the nucleus — your skeleton, holding everything together without adding any charge of its own. The electron carries a negative charge and is found outside the nucleus entirely, moving through a region called the electron cloud — your circulation, constantly moving through the outer layers of the body. The nucleus itself is tiny — if an atom were blown up to the size of your whole body, the nucleus would be about the size of a single grain of rice at your core — but that grain holds almost every bit of the atom's actual mass, since a proton or neutron weighs roughly 1,800 times as much as a single electron. Nearly all of an atom's actual size is empty space in the electron cloud around that one dense core.

2What makes one element different from every other element

Every element has one detail that's genuinely its own — one no other element ever shares. That's the atomic number: the exact number of protons packed into an atom's nucleus. Change the proton count, and you've changed which element you're looking at entirely — no two elements ever share the same atomic number, so it works like an ID that never repeats twice. An element is a pure substance made up of only one type of atom — meaning every single atom of that element carries that one exact atomic number, no exceptions.

Take three real cases. Hydrogen has exactly 1 proton in its nucleus — atomic number 1, the simplest atom there is. Carbon has exactly 6 protons — atomic number 6. Oxygen has exactly 8 protons — atomic number 8. Count the protons in the nucleus, and you've read the ID — you know exactly which element you're looking at. One more detail closes the file: a neutral atom (no leftover charge) always carries the exact same number of electrons as it does protons, so the positive and negative charges balance out to zero overall.

3Why particles don't just sit anywhere they want

None of an atom's layout is random — every particle sits exactly where it does for a real reason. Electrons don't drift anywhere in the electron cloud; they're organized into shells (also called energy levels), stacked at set distances from the nucleus, like blood circulating through set loops instead of just anywhere. The shell closest to the nucleus fills first and can hold a maximum of 2 electrons. Once that first shell is full, the next electrons start filling the second shell, which can hold up to 8. Electrons closest to the nucleus are held there more tightly, at a lower energy level — so an atom always fills its innermost shell completely before any electron goes to the next one out. Take Carbon again: 6 electrons total — the first 2 fill the first shell completely, and the remaining 4 sit in the second shell. Oxygen, with 8 electrons: 2 fill the first shell, and the other 6 sit in the second shell.

The nucleus has its own reason for needing neutrons specifically. Every proton in a nucleus carries the exact same positive charge, and same charges push each other away — so a nucleus packed with several protons would fly apart from that repulsion alone. Neutrons sit in between the protons and hold the nucleus together, acting like a skeleton holding a crowded core together instead of letting it push itself apart. That's exactly why every element past hydrogen needs neutrons in its nucleus, not just protons.

Reading the periodic table — the full chart on every element

The periodic table is the full chart on every known element, all in one place. Every element gets its own square, arranged in order of increasing atomic number — left to right, top to bottom, starting from hydrogen at atomic number 1. Each row is called a period: every time a new outer electron shell starts, a new period begins. Each column is called a group (or family): elements stacked in the same column tend to share similar properties, because they carry a similar number of electrons in their outermost shell.

History: how we found out bodies this small even existed

1803
John Dalton proposes that every element is made of identical atoms of that one element, and different elements are made of different atoms — the first real, testable atomic theory.
1897
J.J. Thomson discovers the electron — the first subatomic particle ever found, proving atoms aren't truly indivisible. He pictures the atom as a "plum pudding": a positive blob studded with negative electrons.
1911
Ernest Rutherford's gold foil experiment finds that an atom's positive charge and nearly all its mass are packed into one tiny, dense center — the nucleus — overturning the plum-pudding model completely.
1913
Niels Bohr proposes that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed shells at set distances, like planets in orbit — the model this whole reading is built on.

Real-world application: doctors read your own hydrogen atoms

An MRI scanner doesn't cut you open to see inside — it actually detects the protons inside the hydrogen atoms that make up most of the water in your body. Since a human body is roughly 60% water, doctors can build a complete picture of what's happening inside you just by reading the signal those hydrogen protons give off while sitting inside a powerful magnetic field. The exact same particle this whole reading is built on is quite literally what makes that scan possible.
