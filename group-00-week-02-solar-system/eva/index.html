Day 1 · Case Briefing

Read the Evidence the Solar System Leaves Behind

You can't solve a case without knowing what counts as evidence. This briefing covers the full picture: what a star actually is, how the eight planets differ, why gravity holds everything in its place, and what a day and a year really mean.

Every case starts with the biggest suspect in the room

Before I can investigate a single planet, I need to understand the one object every planet in this case is connected to. It sits in the middle of the whole scene, and every other clue in this investigation points back to it.

1Suspect zero: the star at the center of it all

The Sun is a star. A star is a giant ball of hot gas — so hot that it glows and makes its own light and its own heat, unlike a planet or a moon, which only shine because they reflect light from somewhere else. The Sun sits in the very middle of the whole case, and it is by far the biggest, heaviest object in it — more than a million Earths could fit inside it. Every single suspect in this investigation, without exception, moves around the Sun.

The Sun is made almost entirely of two gases, hydrogen and helium. It's so enormous that light leaving its surface still takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth — even though light is the fastest thing there is. Every bit of warmth, and almost every bit of light, that reaches any suspect in this case starts here.

2Eight suspects, one lineup, in order

Every planet in our solar system is built one of two basic ways, and where it sits in line from the Sun is never random:

🪨 Mercury

Smallest planet, and the closest to the Sun. Rocky and covered in craters, with no moons at all.

🪨 Venus

Similar size to Earth, but wrapped in thick, toxic clouds that trap heat — making it the hottest planet, even hotter than Mercury.

🪨 Earth

The only planet known to have liquid water on its surface, and the only one known to have life.

🪨 Mars

Called the "Red Planet" because rusty red dust covers its rocky surface.

☁️ Jupiter

The biggest planet in the solar system by far — a gas giant with a giant swirling storm called the Great Red Spot.

☁️ Saturn

A gas giant famous for its wide, bright rings, made of countless chunks of ice and rock.

☁️ Uranus

A gas giant tipped almost completely on its side, so it spins more like a rolling ball than a spinning top.

☁️ Neptune

The windiest planet, and the farthest from the Sun — a deep blue gas giant.

The four planets closest to the Sun — Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars — are rocky planets. Each one has a hard, solid surface made of rock, the same kind of ground you could actually stand on. The four planets farthest from the Sun — Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune — are gas giants. A gas giant is made mostly of gas, all the way through, with no solid surface at all — and every one of them is far bigger than any rocky planet. Between the rocky planets and the gas giants, in the gap between Mars and Jupiter, sits the asteroid belt — more on that later.

3The invisible force holding every suspect in line

Every planet is trying to move in a straight line, forever, out into space. The only reason it doesn't is gravity — an invisible pulling force. The Sun is so big and so heavy that its gravity reaches out and pulls on every planet, all the time, curving each planet's path into a loop instead of a straight line. That curved path a planet follows around the Sun is called its orbit. If the Sun's gravity ever switched off, every planet would stop curving and fly straight off into space in whatever direction it was already moving.

A year is simply the time it takes a planet to complete one full orbit — one whole trip around the Sun. Planets closer to the Sun have a much shorter path to travel, and they also move faster, so their year is short: Mercury, the closest planet, finishes a full orbit — a whole Mercury year — in only about 88 Earth days. Planets farther from the Sun have a much longer path to travel, and they move more slowly, so their year is long: Neptune, the farthest planet, takes about 165 Earth years to complete just one orbit.

4Every suspect has its own private clock

Besides orbiting the Sun, every planet also rotates — it spins on its own axis, an invisible line running straight through its middle, like a spinning top. A day is the time it takes a planet to complete one full rotation — one full spin. Whichever side of a planet happens to be facing the Sun at any moment gets daytime; the side facing away gets night. Earth completes one full rotation in about 24 hours — that's exactly what an "Earth day" means, and where the length of every day you've ever lived actually comes from.

Not every planet spins at the same speed. Jupiter is enormous, but it spins incredibly fast — one full Jupiter day takes only about 10 hours. Venus is the opposite: it spins so slowly that one single Venus day is actually longer than one whole Venus year.

5Two more suspects: the one who follows, and the ones who never got a job

The Moon is a big, rocky ball that orbits Earth, the same way Earth orbits the Sun — Earth's own gravity pulls on the Moon and holds it in its orbit. The Moon has no light of its own; every bit of its glow in the night sky is really sunlight bouncing off its rocky surface and reflecting back down to us. As the Moon orbits Earth over about a month, we see different amounts of its sunlit half from where we stand — that changing view is what makes the Moon look like it changes shape night to night, from a thin sliver to a full bright circle and back again.

Not every piece of rock in the solar system ever became a planet. Between Mars and Jupiter sits the asteroid belt — a wide band of countless rocky chunks, called asteroids, all orbiting the Sun together. Scientists think these are leftover material that never managed to clump together into a real planet. Farther out, past Neptune, live comets — balls of ice, rock, and dust. Most of the time a comet is just a quiet frozen lump, but when its orbit carries it in close to the Sun, the Sun's heat turns some of its ice straight into gas, streaming off it and forming the long, bright, glowing tail comets are famous for.

History: how we figured out the real orbit evidence

1543
Nicolaus Copernicus publishes the case that the Sun, not Earth, sits at the center of the solar system — overturning what almost everyone had believed for centuries.
1609–1619
Johannes Kepler works out the real math of orbits: planets travel in ovals, not perfect circles, and planets farther from the Sun really do take longer to complete one orbit — exactly the evidence you just used above.
1969
NASA's Apollo 11 mission lands the first humans on the Moon — Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walk on its surface and bring real Moon rock back to Earth as physical evidence.

Real-world application: orbit evidence is a real working case

Real engineers use the exact same gravity-and-orbit evidence you just reviewed to launch satellites that circle Earth for GPS and weather forecasting — each one has to be given exactly the right speed to stay in a stable orbit instead of falling back down or flying off into space. Space agencies also use each planet's real year length to plan when to launch a spacecraft toward Mars or Jupiter, so the planet is actually in the right place in its orbit by the time the spacecraft arrives, sometimes years later.
