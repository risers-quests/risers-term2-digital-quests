Day 1 · Case Briefing

Read the Evidence Every Flower Leaves

You can't solve a case without knowing what counts as evidence. This briefing covers the full picture: what pollination actually is, which features point to which pollinator, why a flower becomes a seed at all, and how that seed ends up somewhere completely new.

Why a flower isn't just decoration

A flower's job was never to look pretty for us. Every color, shape, and smell it has is aimed at one thing: getting pollen from one flower to another, usually with help from a visitor it's trying to attract. Before you can read any flower's evidence correctly, you need to know exactly what pollination is and why a flower would go to all that trouble in the first place.

1What pollination actually is

A flower's male part is the stamen — a stalk called a filament topped with an anther, which makes pollen. A flower's female part is the pistil — a sticky stigma on top of a tube called the style, leading down to the ovary, where the ovules wait. Pollination is simply pollen moving from an anther to a stigma — nothing more, nothing less.

Self-pollination is pollen moving within the same flower or plant. Cross-pollination is pollen moving between two different plants of the same species — and it's the one most flowers are actually built for, since it produces stronger, more varied offspring. That's the whole motive behind every flashy petal and every strong smell: getting a visitor to carry pollen somewhere else.

🔍 Witness Statement — something doesn't add up

A witness statement in your case file reads: "This flower is bright red, shaped like a long tube, and packed with sweet nectar deep inside — it must be pollinated by wind." Something in that statement is wrong. What is it, and what should the witness have said instead?

2Meet the suspects: who pollinates what, and why

Every regular visitor to a flower is drawn in by specific evidence that flower is deliberately leaving behind:

🐝 Bees

Drawn to blue, purple, and yellow. Can see UV patterns invisible to us that act like a landing-strip arrow straight to the nectar. Flowers offer a flat "landing pad" petal shape.

🦋 Butterflies

Drawn to red, orange, and pink. Need a landing platform, and reach nectar deep in narrow tube-shaped flowers with their long proboscis.

🐦 Hummingbirds & sunbirds

Drawn to red, tube-shaped flowers with lots of nectar deep inside. No landing pad needed — they hover. Poor sense of smell, so scent doesn't matter to them.

🦇 Moths & bats

Night visitors. Flowers stay pale or white (visible in low light) and give off a strong sweet scent after dark, since color alone won't get noticed in the dark.

A smaller set of flowers go for a stranger strategy: some flies and beetles are drawn to flowers that mimic rotting meat — dull brown or purple, with a genuinely foul smell — because that's exactly what those insects are already looking for.

Wind pollination plays an entirely different game — grasses, wheat, corn, and most trees don't bother attracting anyone. Their flowers are small, dull, scentless, and produce no nectar at all, since there's no visitor to reward. Instead they release huge amounts of very light pollen into the air and grow large, feathery stigmas built to catch whatever drifts past. Water pollination is rare, found mostly in aquatic plants whose pollen simply floats or drifts through the water itself.

3From flower to seed

Once pollen lands on a stigma, it grows a microscopic tube down through the style to reach an ovule waiting in the ovary — this is fertilization. Every fertilized ovule becomes a seed, holding a tiny baby plant plus a food store to get it started. The ovary around it swells into a fruit — which, botanically, includes things you'd never call "fruit" in a kitchen: a pea pod, a maple's spinning "key," and a dandelion's fluffy puff are all technically fruits.

4Why a seed can't just stay put

If a seed dropped straight down and grew right under its own parent, it would be fighting that same parent — and every sibling seed beside it — for the exact same limited sunlight, water, and soil nutrients. It would also land in ground already carrying whatever pests or diseases the parent plant attracts. Traveling gives a seed a genuine shot at growing without that competition, and lets the whole species spread into new territory it couldn't otherwise reach.

🎓 Rookie Briefing

A new detective on your team asks why a plant doesn't just drop its seeds right underneath itself, since that seems so much easier. Explain it in two or three sentences, simple enough for them to repeat back correctly.

5Five ways a seed leaves the scene

💨 Wind

A dandelion's fluffy white pappus catches the breeze. A maple seed's "wing" spins like a helicopter blade, slowing its fall so wind carries it further. Orchid seeds are almost dust-light.

🌊 Water

A coconut's thick, waterproof, fibrous husk lets it float across an ocean for months and still sprout on a new shore. Mangrove seeds float upright and root the instant they touch mud.

🐾 Hitchhiking

Burdock and cocklebur seeds are covered in tiny hooks that catch on fur or clothing and drop off far from the parent — the exact trick that inspired Velcro.

🍒 Eaten & carried

Berries and cherries are bright and sweet specifically to get eaten. The seed survives the gut undigested and is deposited elsewhere, with a bit of natural fertilizer.

A fifth method needs no outside help at all: explosive dispersal. A ripe pea pod, or a touch-me-not seed pod, dries out and builds up tension until it suddenly splits and flings its seeds outward — a squirting cucumber can launch its seeds several metres this way.

💨 One gust, and it's gone
A dandelion seed's whole design is built around catching exactly this moment.

History: how we learned to read the evidence

1793
Christian Konrad Sprengel publishes the first real case that flowers are shaped specifically to attract particular pollinators — a genuinely new idea at the time.
1862
Charles Darwin studies a Madagascar orchid with an extraordinarily long nectar spur and predicts a moth with an equally long tongue must exist to pollinate it — years before anyone ever saw one.
1903
Darwin's predicted moth, Xanthopan morganii praedicta, is finally described — but not actually photographed pollinating that exact orchid until 1992, confirming his case file 130 years later.
1941
Swiss engineer George de Mestral examines burdock burrs stuck to his dog's fur under a microscope, studies the tiny hooks, and invents Velcro — copying a seed's own evidence directly.

Real-world application: this evidence runs the world's food supply

Roughly a third of everything humans eat depends on animal pollination, mostly bees — commercial growers of crops like almonds truck millions of beehives across entire countries just to have them in the right orchard at the right week. "Colony collapse," where bee populations crash, is treated as a genuine food-security case by governments, not a minor concern. Vanilla is an even sharper example: outside its native Mexico, the vanilla orchid has no natural pollinator at all, so nearly all vanilla today is hand-pollinated, flower by flower, by real workers — a huge part of why real vanilla costs so much. And Velcro — hook-and-loop fastener used on shoes, bags, medical equipment, even spacesuits — is a direct, working copy of a burr's hitchhiking hooks, still on the job decades later.

    Day 3 · Close the Case

    Present Your Findings

    Real detectives present evidence, not vibes. Here's how to open strong and cover everything that matters.

    Your opening move

    Don't say "I'm going to talk about pollination." Instead, hold up your flower model and ask the room: "Who do you think visits this flower — and what's your evidence?" Let a few guesses land before you reveal your actual case.

    What to include

    • Which flower and pollinator you investigated, and why you chose that pair
    • Every labeled feature on your evidence board and what it proves
    • One thing that surprised you while building the evidence
    • Your honest self-check — the part you're least sure about, and why
    Facilitator only

    If Eva's evidence board has a shaky or uncertain piece, that's the best moment of the presentation, not a flaw to smooth over — ask her directly "what would make you more sure about that one?" A detective who can name what she doesn't know yet is showing real understanding, not a gap in it.
