Mission Meridian-2: Earth to Mars Transfer

Meridian-2 is a probe, not a crewed ship — no life-support margin to fall back on. Every leg of its transfer to Mars runs on real physics: Newton's law of gravitation, force and acceleration, orbital velocity, escape velocity, free fall, and momentum. Get the physics wrong, and the probe doesn't just "fail a question" — it drifts, stalls, or burns fuel it will never get back.

Part 1 · The Pull That Never Lets Go

Gravity and the Inverse-Square Law

Before you can fly a real transfer orbit, you need the one force that shapes every leg of it: gravity. Not "things fall down" — the actual relationship mission control uses to predict exactly how hard gravity pulls, at any distance.

1What gravity actually is

Every object with mass pulls on every other object with mass — that's not a figure of speech, it's literally what Newton's law of universal gravitation says. You feel Earth's pull because Earth is enormous and close. You don't feel the Moon's pull, even though the Moon is genuinely massive, because it's much farther away. You don't feel a person standing next to you pulling on you at all, because a person's mass is far too small to produce a pull worth noticing. The strength of the pull between any two objects always comes down to the same two things: how massive they both are, and how far apart they are. More mass means a stronger pull. More distance means a weaker pull. That's the whole law — and it's the exact same one real mission control uses today to plot a probe's path to another planet.

2Why distance matters so much

Here's the part that trips people up: gravity's pull doesn't just weaken with distance — it weakens with the square of distance. That relationship is called an inverse-square law. Move an object twice as far from a planet, and the pull isn't cut in half — it's cut to a quarter (2² = 4, so the pull is 1/4 as strong). Move it three times as far, and the pull doesn't drop to a third — it drops to a ninth (3² = 9). This is why a spacecraft that seems to be crawling away from a planet can lose most of that planet's pull surprisingly fast, and why getting the distance factor right — and remembering to square it — is the single most common place this kind of calculation goes wrong.

Part 2 · Thrust and the Orbit It Buys You

Force, Acceleration & Orbital Velocity

Gravity explains the pull. This part covers the other half: how a spacecraft's own engines fight back against it, and what it actually takes to stay in a stable orbit instead of falling in or flying off.

3Newton's second law: force from acceleration

Every real thruster burn comes down to one equation: force = mass × acceleration (F = ma). Acceleration itself is just how fast velocity is changing — the change in velocity (Δv) divided by the time it takes. Put those together, and a propulsion engineer sizing a burn always works the same two-step chain: find the acceleration needed first (Δv ÷ time), then multiply by the spacecraft's mass to get the force the thrusters actually have to produce. A heavier spacecraft needs more force to hit the exact same acceleration as a lighter one — mass doesn't change the law, it changes how much force the law demands.

⚙️ Quick Check

Two identical thrusters each need to produce the same acceleration, but Probe A is twice as heavy as Probe B. Which thruster has to produce more force, and why, based on Newton's second law?

4Orbital velocity: falling and missing

An orbit is one of the most counterintuitive ideas in this whole rotation: an orbiting object is constantly falling toward whatever it orbits — gravity never once switches off. What keeps it from actually hitting the surface is sideways speed. It's moving forward fast enough that by the time gravity has pulled it down some, the ground has curved away underneath it by roughly the same amount — so it keeps "falling" and keeps "missing," forever, tracing a closed loop. That sideways speed is orbital velocity, and it's just distance ÷ time applied to a circular path instead of a straight line: the distance all the way around one orbit, divided by the time it takes to complete one full lap. A tighter, faster orbit and a wider, slower one can both be perfectly stable — what has to be true is that the sideways speed exactly balances how hard gravity is pulling inward at that particular distance.

Part 3 · Breaking Free, Falling Back

Escape Velocity & Free Fall

One last pair of ideas before you fly the actual mission: what it takes to leave a planet's gravity behind for good, and what happens to something that doesn't — it just falls.

5Escape velocity

Escape velocity is the minimum speed an object needs to break completely free of a planet's gravity, permanently, with no further engine burns needed once it's reached. A more massive planet holds onto everything near it with a stronger pull, so it takes a genuinely higher speed to escape it — that's why leaving a small body like the Moon takes far less speed than leaving Earth, and leaving a giant planet like Jupiter takes far more speed than leaving Earth. Escape velocity isn't the same thing as orbital velocity — orbital velocity keeps you circling a planet forever; escape velocity is what it takes to never come back at all.

6Free fall under real gravity

An object in free fall — falling with nothing but gravity acting on it, no air resistance factored in — doesn't fall at a constant speed. It accelerates the entire way down, at a fixed rate. On Earth, that rate is g = 9.8 m/s², meaning its speed increases by 9.8 meters per second for every single second it keeps falling. One second in, it's moving 9.8 m/s. Two seconds in, 19.6 m/s. Four seconds in, nearly 40 m/s. That steady, compounding acceleration — not a constant speed — is exactly why a fall that starts slow ends fast, and it's the same real number engineers use to predict drop-test impacts and how fast a dropped tool would actually be moving when it hits the ground.

    Part 5 · Build Day

    Build a Momentum-Collision Track

    Leg 6 of the mission ran on momentum conservation — mass times velocity, added up before a collision, has to equal mass times velocity added up after. Today you test that on your own table with real objects, real distances, and a real stopwatch.

    Why this build matters

    Real thruster burns work on exactly this principle in reverse: a spacecraft throws exhaust mass one way, so momentum conservation forces the spacecraft to move the other way — that's the ONLY reason a rocket engine works at all in the vacuum of space, where there's nothing to "push against" except its own exhaust. Watching momentum transfer between two rolling balls is the same law, just visible.
