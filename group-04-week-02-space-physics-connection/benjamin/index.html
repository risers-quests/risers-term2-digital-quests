Mission Meridian-2: Earth to Mars Transfer

Meridian-2 is a probe, not a crewed ship — no life-support margin to fall back on. Every leg of the transfer runs on real physics: Newton's law of gravitation, force and acceleration, orbital velocity, escape velocity, free fall, and momentum. Get the physics wrong, and the probe doesn't just "fail a question" — it drifts, stalls, or burns fuel it will never get back.

Orbital Mechanics Officer: Benjamin

You're the mission's orbital mechanics officer for Meridian-2's transfer to Mars. This rotation goes a level past the basic gravity-and-orbits picture: you'll use Newton's inverse-square law to predict how gravity's pull actually changes with distance, connect force to acceleration the way real propulsion engineers do, and work through the same momentum math that governs every real spacecraft maneuver. Day 2 you build a physical momentum-collision track to see the same conservation law in action on your own table. Day 3, you brief mission control on how the transfer went.

    Day 2 · Build Day

    Build a Momentum-Collision Track

    Leg 6 of the mission ran on momentum conservation — mass times velocity, added up before a collision, has to equal mass times velocity added up after. Today you test that on your own table with real objects, real distances, and a real stopwatch.

    Why this build matters

    Real thruster burns work on exactly this principle in reverse: a spacecraft throws exhaust mass one way, so momentum conservation forces the spacecraft to move the other way — that's the ONLY reason a rocket engine works at all in the vacuum of space, where there's nothing to "push against" except its own exhaust. Watching momentum transfer between two rolling balls is the same law, just visible.
