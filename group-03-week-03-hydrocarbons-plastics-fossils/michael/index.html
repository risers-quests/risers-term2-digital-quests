Day 1 · Systems Briefing

Fossil Fuels, Hydrocarbons, and Plastics — the Real Chain

Three real materials, one continuous chain, and a real trade-off underneath all of it. This briefing covers exactly how fossil fuels form and what they actually turn into, before you build something real of your own.

1Hydrocarbons: molecules built from carbon and hydrogen

Say the word "hydrocarbon" and it sounds like it could be almost anything. It isn't. A hydrocarbon is a molecule built from exactly two elements: carbon and hydrogen, and nothing else. The simplest one is methane (CH₄) — one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms. Real hydrocarbons range from that single-carbon molecule all the way up to long chains of dozens of carbon atoms strung together, each one still built from only those same two elements.

Hydrocarbons don't just float around loose in nature. They're found packed inside three real fossil fuels: crude oil, natural gas, and coal. Crude oil is actually a mixture of thousands of different hydrocarbon molecules of different chain lengths, which is exactly why it gets separated — refined — into different products like gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel. Natural gas is mostly methane, the simplest hydrocarbon there is. Coal is a little different: it's still a fossil fuel and still carbon-rich, but it isn't a clean hydrocarbon mixture the way oil and gas are — real coal also carries other elements like sulfur and nitrogen mixed into its structure.

🛢️ Crude oil

A liquid mixture of many different hydrocarbons, pumped from source rock and refined into fuels and petrochemicals.

🔥 Natural gas

Mostly methane (CH₄), the simplest hydrocarbon — one carbon atom, four hydrogen atoms.

⚫ Coal

A carbon-rich solid formed from ancient swamp plants — less purely hydrocarbon than oil or gas, but still a fossil fuel.

2How fossils and fossil fuels actually form

Fossil fuels don't form the way a lot of people picture it — there's no single "dinosaur soup" underground. Oil and natural gas form mostly from ancient marine organisms: tiny plankton and algae that lived, died, and sank to ancient seafloors by the trillions. Buried under layer after layer of sediment, that organic material turned into a waxy substance called kerogen. Buried deep enough — thousands of feet down — heat and pressure slowly convert kerogen into the hydrocarbons that make up oil and natural gas, collecting in porous source rock.

Coal forms through a related but separate path. Instead of marine plankton, it starts with ancient swamp and forest plant material, piling up faster than it can fully decompose in low-oxygen swamp water. Buried and compressed over millions of years, that plant material moves through real stages — peat, then lignite, then bituminous coal, then anthracite — as depth, heat, and pressure all increase.

None of this happens on any timescale a person can watch. Real source rock and real coal seams take millions of years to form. That's exactly why fossil fuels are called a non-renewable resource: humans are pumping and mining them out far, far faster than the Earth could ever replace them.

Four stages, each one deeper, older, and hotter than the one above it.

3What plastics actually are

Plastic isn't dug up or mined the way coal is — it's manufactured. A plastic is a synthetic polymer: a long chain molecule built by chemically bonding together thousands of small repeating units called monomers. Most conventional plastic starts as crude oil or natural gas, refined and processed into petrochemicals like ethylene and propylene — small hydrocarbon molecules that then get linked together into those long polymer chains.

Different starting monomers make different plastics with different real properties. Polyethylene (from ethylene) is lightweight and flexible — bags and bottles. Polypropylene (from propylene) is stiffer and handles heat better — bottle caps and food containers. PVC (from vinyl chloride) is rigid and weather-resistant — pipes and siding. What makes plastic so widely used across all of these types is a real, practical combination: it's durable, lightweight, cheap to produce at scale, and it can be molded into almost any shape while it's still soft.

🧵 Polyethylene (PE)

The most common plastic by weight — bags, bottles, and film. Built from ethylene monomers.

🪣 Polypropylene (PP)

Stiffer and more heat-resistant — bottle caps, food containers, rope.

🚰 PVC

Polyvinyl chloride — rigid and weather-resistant — pipes, siding, some packaging.

4Put together, it's a system — with a real trade-off

Trace the whole chain and it's one continuous system, not three separate topics: a fossil fuel (oil, gas, or coal) is buried, ancient organic material; inside it are hydrocarbon molecules; refine and chemically bond those hydrocarbons together and you get a plastic. Every plastic item within reach right now traces back through that exact same chain.

That chain also carries a real trade-off. The properties that make plastic so useful — cheap, lightweight, and built to last — are the exact same properties that make it an environmental problem once it's thrown away. A plastic bottle that resists breaking down for years on a shelf also resists breaking down for centuries in a landfill or the ocean, where it instead breaks into smaller and smaller pieces called microplastics. Burning fossil fuels for energy also releases carbon dioxide, adding to climate change. Recycling helps, but it doesn't fully solve the problem: different plastic types can't all be recycled together, quality degrades each time plastic is reprocessed, and a large share of plastic waste still isn't recycled at all — which is exactly why reducing how much gets used in the first place, and developing real alternatives like biodegradable plastics, matters as much as recycling does.

Real-world application: this chain gets worked every day

Petroleum geologists study rock layers to find where source rock actually sits, before anyone drills a single well. Petrochemical engineers run the crackers and reactors that turn crude oil and natural gas into the ethylene and propylene that become real plastic products. Materials scientists test which resin type a product should actually be made from, balancing cost, durability, and how easily it can be recycled afterward. Paleontologists use the same buried-and-preserved idea, on a different material, to read Earth's ancient history from actual fossils. None of this is guesswork — every one of those jobs starts from the same real chain you're about to trace yourself.

History: how we found and used this chain

~300 mya
During the Carboniferous Period, vast swamp forests cover much of the Earth. Dead plant material piles up faster than it can fully decompose in the swamps' low-oxygen water — the start of most of the coal deposits still being mined today.
1859
Edwin Drake drills the first commercial oil well in Titusville, Pennsylvania, launching the modern petroleum industry and the search for buried source rock worldwide.
1907
Leo Baekeland invents Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic — made entirely from fossil-fuel-derived chemicals, not adapted from any natural material like earlier plastics had been.

      📖 Glossary

      Hydrocarbon
      A molecule made only of carbon and hydrogen atoms — the building block of fossil fuels and most plastics.
      Fossil fuel
      An energy resource — oil, natural gas, or coal — formed from the buried remains of ancient living things over millions of years.
      Fossil
      The preserved remains or traces of an ancient organism — bones, shells, or footprints — found in rock.
      Kerogen
      The waxy, carbon-rich material inside buried source rock that heat and pressure slowly convert into oil and natural gas.
      Crude oil
      The raw, unrefined liquid mixture of hydrocarbons pumped from the ground, before it's separated into fuels and other products.
      Polymer
      A long chain molecule made of many smaller repeating units, called monomers, bonded together.
      Monomer
      A single small molecule that bonds with many others like it to form a long chain called a polymer.
      Petrochemical
      A chemical made from crude oil or natural gas, used as a raw ingredient for products like plastic.
      Non-renewable resource
      A resource that forms so slowly, over millions of years, that using it up happens far faster than nature can replace it.
      Recycling
      Reprocessing a used material into new material instead of throwing it away — though not every plastic type can be recycled the same way.
