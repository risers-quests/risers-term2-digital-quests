Day 1 · Orientation — The Pump Station

Before You Touch a Patient, Know the Pump

Dr. Vasquez meets you at the nurses' station with a plastic model heart in one hand. "Sit. Before you touch a monitor, a chart, or a patient, you're going to know this cold."

Why a cadet starts here, not in the ER

Every cell in the body needs a steady delivery of oxygen and nutrients, and a steady removal of waste like carbon dioxide. That whole job — transport — is run by the circulatory system: the heart, the blood, and the blood vessels that carry it everywhere. When something goes wrong with a patient's heart, a responder who doesn't know the pump's actual layout is guessing. Today, you learn the layout.

📹 Circulatory System & the Pathway of Blood

1The heart's four rooms

The heart is about the size of your own closed fist and weighs roughly 225–340 grams. It sits in the center of the chest cavity, tipped slightly to the left, enclosed in a double-layered protective sac called the pericardium — the fluid between its two layers (pericardial fluid) cushions the heart against shocks and jerks, the way packing foam protects something fragile in a box.

Internally, a muscular wall called the septum splits the heart into a right half and a left half — and blood on one side never mixes with blood on the other. Each half is further split into an upper chamber, the atrium (plural: atria), and a lower chamber, the ventricle. That's four chambers total: two atria, two ventricles. The atria are the "receiving docks" — smaller, thin-walled, they just catch incoming blood. The ventricles are the "distribution engines" — bigger, thick-walled, muscular enough to actually push blood out to the lungs or the entire rest of the body.

2One-way gates: the valves

Blood only ever flows one direction through the heart, and four valves enforce that. Between the right atrium and right ventricle sits the tricuspid valve (three flaps). Between the left atrium and left ventricle sits the bicuspid valve, also called the mitral valve (two flaps) — together these are the atrio-ventricular (AV) valves. At the two exits where blood leaves the ventricles — into the pulmonary trunk and into the aorta — sit the semilunar valves, shaped like little half-moons, which stop blood from flowing backward into the ventricles once it's been pushed out.

3The pump's own conductor

The heart is built of specialized cardiac muscle that contracts and relaxes continuously, without ever resting, for an entire lifetime — about 72 times a minute. That rhythm isn't random: it's triggered by an electrical impulse that originates in a small node in the upper right atrium called the sinoatrial node, or SA node. Because it sets the timing for every single heartbeat, it's nicknamed the heart's natural pacemaker. If a person's SA node is damaged or the heart's electrical conduction has problems, doctors can surgically place an artificial pacemaker — a small mechanical device that takes over the job of keeping the rhythm steady.

History: how we learned to read the pump

1628
William Harvey publishes De Motu Cordis, the first accurate description of blood circulating in one closed loop, pushed by the heart as a pump — overturning over a thousand years of the wrong theory before it.
1816
René Laennec invents the stethoscope, letting doctors listen to a heart's valves closing for the first time without pressing an ear directly to a patient's chest.
1900
Karl Landsteiner identifies the ABO blood group system — the discovery that makes safe blood transfusion possible for the first time in history (this week's Day 4 case runs entirely on his work).
1903
Willem Einthoven builds the first practical ECG machine, letting doctors literally see the SA node's electrical signal on paper.
1958
Engineer Rune Elmqvist and surgeon Åke Senning implant the first artificial pacemaker in a human patient, Arne Larsson — who went on to outlive both of them.

Day 2 · Dispatch — Tracing Every Route

The Highway Network the Pump Feeds

"Knowing the pump gets you nowhere if you don't know the roads it pumps through," Dr. Vasquez says, pulling up a full-body diagram on the wall monitor. "Today, you learn the network."

4Three kinds of road

Blood vessels form a network of three types. Arteries carry blood away from the heart to the rest of the body — blood inside them moves fast, under high pressure, and arteries have no valves (they don't need them; the heart's own pumping force keeps blood moving forward). Veins carry blood back toward the heart — blood inside them moves slower, under lower pressure, so veins have valves along their length to stop blood from sliding backward. Capillaries are the finest vessels of all, so thin-walled that oxygen, carbon dioxide, water, and nutrients can actually pass straight through their walls — they're the real exchange point between blood and every body tissue.

🚦 Cadet Check

Name the real difference between an artery and a vein in terms of BOTH the pressure/speed of the blood inside it AND whether it has valves.

5The named routes

A handful of specific vessels matter most. The superior vena cava and inferior vena cava are the two big veins bringing deoxygenated blood into the right atrium — superior from the upper body, inferior from the lower body and legs. The pulmonary trunk carries deoxygenated blood out of the right ventricle toward the lungs, branching into a left and right pulmonary artery. Once the lungs have oxygenated it, the pulmonary veins carry that blood back into the left atrium. The aorta — the single largest artery in the body — leaves the left ventricle, carrying oxygenated blood out to every other part of the body through its branches. And the heart feeds its own muscular walls through the coronary artery (a branch straight off the aorta) and drains itself through the coronary veins, which empty into the right atrium.

6Double circulation: the two-loop delivery system

Blood actually passes through the heart twice to complete one full lap of the body — that's called double circulation. The first loop is pulmonary circulation: deoxygenated blood leaves the right ventricle, travels to the lungs to pick up oxygen, and returns to the left atrium. The second loop is systemic circulation: that now-oxygenated blood leaves the left ventricle through the aorta, travels to every other part of the body, drops off oxygen and nutrients, picks up carbon dioxide and waste, and returns to the right atrium as deoxygenated blood again — ready to start the whole cycle over.

Real-world application: this network runs real medicine

A blood pressure cuff measures the actual force blood exerts on artery walls — the same pressure this section just described. A stethoscope lets a doctor listen for the exact valve-closing sounds Day 1 covered. An ECG machine reads the SA node's electrical signal directly off a patient's skin. A blocked coronary artery is exactly what causes a heart attack — treated today with procedures like angioplasty or coronary bypass surgery, both of which are literally rerouting or reopening the coronary highway this section named. None of this is abstract; it's the network a cardiac cadet works with every single day.

Day 3 · Build — The Second Network

The Backup Network, Then a Real Model of Both

"There's a second transport network running alongside the one you just learned," Dr. Vasquez says, "quieter, slower, and just as important. Learn it — then build me a model of all of it, in three colors."

7The parallel network: lymph

The body actually has three principal fluids: blood, contained in the heart and blood vessels; tissue fluid, which leaks out of capillary walls to bathe every cell directly; and lymph, formed when some of that tissue fluid drains into a separate set of minute channels called lymph vessels, which run alongside the blood vessels. Lymph is pale yellow, contains no red blood cells, and moves only when surrounding muscles contract and squeeze it along — it has no pump of its own like the heart. Along the way, lymph passes through lymph nodes, which filter it and help localize infections, and lymphatic organs like the spleen, tonsils, and thymus. Eventually all of it drains back into major veins near the right atrium through the thoracic duct, rejoining the blood.

Lymph does real work: it's nutritive (delivers nutrients and oxygen to spots blood can't directly reach), it handles drainage (clears excess tissue fluid and returns proteins to the blood), it handles absorption (fats from the intestine are absorbed through lymph vessels, not blood vessels), and it's part of the body's defence — lymphocytes and monocytes in the lymph fight infection, which is exactly why a swollen lymph node in your groin or armpit is a real, meaningful sign your body is fighting something off.

    Day 4 · Code Amber — The Transfusion Call

    Blood Groups, and a Real Call to Get Right

    Mid-morning, a real call comes in — a delivery cyclist hit by a car, losing blood fast. Dr. Vasquez hands you the chart. "Before the blood bank even answers, tell me who in this room can actually give him blood."

    8Blood groups: the ABO system

    In 1900, Karl Landsteiner discovered that human blood comes in different types, based on proteins called antigens found on the surface of red blood cells. There are two antigen types — A and B. A person is blood group A if their red blood cells carry the A antigen, group B if they carry the B antigen, group AB if they carry both, and group O if they carry neither.

    This matters enormously for transfusion. Blood group O has no antigens at all, so it can safely be given to a person of any blood group — which is why O is called the universal donor. Blood group AB has both antigens already, so a person with AB blood can safely receive from any donor group — which is why AB is called the universal recipient. Group A can give to A or AB; group B can give to B or AB; but A and B can never safely give to each other.

    Real-world application: donation, banks, and safety

    Blood donation is voluntary — a healthy donor gives about one pint (420 mL) at a time. A blood bank stores donated blood, sorted and labeled by group, ready for exactly the kind of emergency Marcus is in right now. Every real transfusion, anywhere in the world, is checked against this same ABO compatibility chart before a single drop is given — it's not a formality, it's the difference between saving a patient and causing a dangerous immune reaction.

    Day 5 · Code Blue

    The Real Emergency, Then Defend It Live

    Friday afternoon. You're refilling your water bottle in the hospital café when someone two tables over suddenly goes silent mid-sentence and slumps out of his chair. Everyone looks at you.

    9When the pump itself is the emergency

    Palpitations — the heart beating too hard, too fast, or skipping a beat — are usually harmless, often caused by stress, anxiety, or certain foods, and often pass on their own. Hypertension (high blood pressure) is blood flowing through the vessels with more force than normal — over time it can strain the heart, damage vessels, and raise the risk of a heart attack or stroke.

    A heart attack is a sudden interruption of blood supply to the heart muscle itself, usually from a blood clot blocking flow through a coronary artery — part of the cardiac muscle starts to die, but the heart itself keeps beating and sending blood around the body. Common symptoms include chest pressure or pain that can spread to the shoulders, neck, jaw, or arms, shortness of breath, dizziness, sweating, and nausea.

    A cardiac arrest is a completely different emergency: the heart itself suddenly stops pumping blood around the body at all. The person will suddenly lose consciousness and stop breathing normally, often with no warning symptoms first. Left untreated for even a few minutes, it's usually fatal. The most common underlying cause is coronary artery disease — the same blocked-artery problem behind a heart attack — but the two conditions are not the same thing, and a heart attack puts a person at high risk of a cardiac arrest happening next.

    If you witness either one, the real first-aid sequence is: call an ambulance or a doctor immediately; help the person into a comfortable sitting or lying position to reduce strain on the heart; if they're unresponsive and not breathing normally, begin chest compressions (pushing on the chest to keep blood circulating manually until help arrives) and, if trained, rescue breathing so the lungs keep getting oxygen. Quick first aid measurably raises a person's real chance of survival.
